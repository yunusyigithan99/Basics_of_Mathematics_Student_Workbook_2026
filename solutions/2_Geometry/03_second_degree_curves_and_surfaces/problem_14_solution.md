# Problem 14 Solution: Cone and Cylinder

## 1. Original Problem
Compare the two second-degree surfaces in $\mathbb{R}^3$:
$$S_1 : x^2 + y^2 = z^2 \quad \text{and} \quad S_2 : x^2 + y^2 = 4.$$
For each surface:
1. Describe the horizontal cross-sections by planes $z = c$.
2. Describe the vertical cross-sections by the coordinate planes $x = 0$ and $y = 0$.
3. Provide a qualitative sketch and describe the global geometric shape.
4. Explain why the radius of the cross-section depends on $z$ in the first surface, but is strictly constant in the second surface.

---

## 2. What We Are Trying to Find
We need to:
1. Identify both surfaces by name (circular double cone vs. circular cylinder).
2. Compute the horizontal traces $z = c$ for both surfaces and express their radii as functions of $c$.
3. Compute the vertical traces $x = 0$ and $y = 0$ for both surfaces.
4. Explain the algebraic and geometric distinction between a surface containing explicit $z$-dependence and a cylindrical surface where $z$ is a completely free, unconstrained variable.

---

## 3. Key Concepts and Theorems
1. **Right Circular Cone:**
   An equation $x^2 + y^2 = k^2 z^2$ represents a circular double cone with apex at the origin $(0, 0, 0)$ whose opening angle is determined by $k$.
2. **Right Circular Cylinder:**
   An equation $x^2 + y^2 = R^2$ in 3-dimensional space contains no $z$-variable. The variable $z$ is free to take any value in $(-\infty, \infty)$, meaning the 2D circle $x^2 + y^2 = R^2$ is extruded infinitely along the direction of the $z$-axis.
3. **Homogeneity and Scaling:**
   In $x^2 + y^2 = z^2$, all terms have degree 2; multiplying $(x, y, z)$ by any scalar $t$ preserves the equation: $(tx)^2 + (ty)^2 = (tz)^2$. This implies the surface is a union of straight lines passing through the origin (a ruled conical surface).

---

## 4. Detailed Step-by-Step Solution

### Step 1: Analyze Surface 1 ($S_1 : x^2 + y^2 = z^2$)

1. **Horizontal cross-sections by $z = c$:**
   Substitute $z = c$:
   $$x^2 + y^2 = c^2.$$
   - For any non-zero height $c \neq 0$, this is a **circle** of radius:
     $$R(c) = \sqrt{c^2} = |c|.$$
     The circle is centered at $(0, 0, c)$ on the $z$-axis.
   - For $c = 0$, $x^2 + y^2 = 0 \implies x = 0, y = 0$, which is the **single point** $(0, 0, 0)$ (the apex of the cone).
2. **Vertical cross-sections by $x = 0$ and $y = 0$:**
   - **Cross-section with $x = 0$ ($yz$-plane):**
     $$0 + y^2 = z^2 \implies z^2 - y^2 = 0 \implies (z - y)(z + y) = 0.$$
     This factors into a **pair of intersecting straight lines**:
     $$z = y \quad \text{and} \quad z = -y.$$
     These lines intersect at the origin at angles of $45^\circ$ to the axes.
   - **Cross-section with $y = 0$ ($xz$-plane):**
     $$x^2 + 0 = z^2 \implies z^2 - x^2 = 0 \implies (z - x)(z + x) = 0.$$
     This factors into a **pair of intersecting straight lines**:
     $$z = x \quad \text{and} \quad z = -x.$$
3. **Geometric Classification of $S_1$:**
   $S_1$ is a **right circular cone** (specifically a double cone), with two symmetric nappes meeting apex-to-apex at the origin $(0, 0, 0)$, one expanding upward for $z > 0$ and one expanding downward for $z < 0$.

---

### Step 2: Analyze Surface 2 ($S_2 : x^2 + y^2 = 4$)

1. **Horizontal cross-sections by $z = c$:**
   Substitute $z = c$:
   $$x^2 + y^2 = 4 = 2^2.$$
   For **every** value of $c \in \mathbb{R}$ (whether $c$ is positive, zero, or negative), the cross-section is an identical **circle** with:
   - Fixed center at $(0, 0, c)$
   - Constant radius:
     $$R(c) \equiv 2.$$
2. **Vertical cross-sections by $x = 0$ and $y = 0$:**
   - **Cross-section with $x = 0$ ($yz$-plane):**
     $$0 + y^2 = 4 \implies y^2 = 4 \implies y = \pm 2.$$
     In the $yz$-plane, this is a **pair of parallel vertical lines**:
     $$y = 2 \quad \text{and} \quad y = -2 \quad (\text{with } z \in \mathbb{R}).$$
   - **Cross-section with $y = 0$ ($xz$-plane):**
     $$x^2 + 0 = 4 \implies x^2 = 4 \implies x = \pm 2.$$
     In the $xz$-plane, this is a **pair of parallel vertical lines**:
     $$x = 2 \quad \text{and} \quad x = -2 \quad (\text{with } z \in \mathbb{R}).$$
3. **Geometric Classification of $S_2$:**
   $S_2$ is a **right circular cylinder** with vertical axis along the $z$-axis, generated by translating a circle of radius 2 parallel to the $z$-axis.

---

### Step 3: Why Radius Depends on $z$ in $S_1$ but Is Constant in $S_2$
- **In $S_1 : x^2 + y^2 = z^2$:**
  The height variable $z$ explicitly couples to the radial distance $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ through the relation $r^2 = z^2 \iff r = |z|$.
  Because $z$ is algebraically present on the right-hand side, any change in elevation $\Delta z$ forces a proportional change in the radius $\Delta r = |\Delta z|$. Geometrically, the generators of the surface are slanted lines meeting at the apex; moving vertically along a slanted line naturally changes the distance to the central axis.
- **In $S_2 : x^2 + y^2 = 4$:**
  The variable $z$ is completely absent from the equation.
  In 3D Cartesian space, an equation omitting a variable imposes no constraint whatsoever on that variable. Thus, if a point $(x, y)$ satisfies $x^2 + y^2 = 4$, the point $(x, y, z)$ automatically satisfies the equation for **all** $z \in (-\infty, \infty)$.
  Geometrically, the generating lines (rulings) are strictly parallel to the $z$-axis; moving vertically moves along parallel straight lines, leaving the distance to the axis constant at $2$.

---

## 5. Why Each Step Is Valid
1. **The difference of squares factorization** $z^2 - y^2 = (z - y)(z + y) = 0$ proves that the vertical cross-sections of the cone are genuine straight lines.
2. **The independence of $z$** in $x^2 + y^2 = 4$ is the definition of a cylinder parallel to the $z$-axis in analytic geometry.
3. **Evaluating $R(c) = |c|$** is valid because the radius is the non-negative square root $\sqrt{c^2} = |c|$.

---

## 6. Final Answer in Words
For the cone $S_1 : x^2 + y^2 = z^2$, horizontal cross-sections $z = c$ are circles of radius $R = |c|$ (and a single point at $c = 0$), while vertical cross-sections $x = 0$ and $y = 0$ are pairs of intersecting diagonal lines $z = \pm y$ and $z = \pm x$. For the cylinder $S_2 : x^2 + y^2 = 4$, horizontal cross-sections are circles of constant radius $R = 2$ for all $z$, while vertical cross-sections are pairs of parallel vertical lines $y = \pm 2$ and $x = \pm 2$. The radius depends on $z$ in $S_1$ because $z^2$ actively couples to $x^2 + y^2$ (forming a slanted conical boundary $r = |z|$), whereas in $S_2$, $z$ is absent, making it an unconstrained variable that extrudes a fixed circle parallel to the vertical axis.

---

## 7. Quick Verification
- Point on cone at $z = 3$: $(0, 3, 3) \implies 0^2 + 3^2 = 3^2 = 9$. Radius is $|3| = 3$.
- Point on cone at $z = -5$: $(3, 4, -5) \implies 3^2 + 4^2 = 25 = (-5)^2$. Radius is $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5 = |-5|$.
- Point on cylinder at $z = 100$: $(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2}, 100) \implies (\sqrt{2})^2 + (\sqrt{2})^2 = 2 + 2 = 4$. Radius is $\sqrt{2+2} = 2$.
- Point on cylinder at $z = -50$: $(0, 2, -50) \implies 0^2 + 2^2 = 4$. Radius is $2$.
Calculations confirm the radius behavior across both surfaces.

---

## 8. Common Mistakes to Avoid
- **Writing $R(c) = c$ instead of $|c|$ for the cone:** Radius is always non-negative; for $z = -3$, the radius is $|-3| = 3$, not $-3$.
- **Thinking $x^2 + y^2 = 4$ is a circle in space:** In 3D space, $x^2 + y^2 = 4$ is a 2D surface (a cylinder), not a 1D curve. To be a circle, it must be intersected with a specific plane $z = c$.
- **Believing the cone only exists for $z \ge 0$:** Forgetting that $z^2 = (-z)^2$ produces a double cone with two symmetric nappes above and below the $xy$-plane.

---

## 9. Classroom Presentation (3-Sentence Summary)
Horizontal slices of $x^2 + y^2 = z^2$ produce circles whose radius equals $|z|$, while vertical coordinate slices yield intersecting diagonal lines $z = \pm y$ and $z = \pm x$, forming a double cone with its apex at the origin. In contrast, $x^2 + y^2 = 4$ lacks the variable $z$ entirely, meaning every horizontal slice is a circle with fixed radius 2 and vertical slices are parallel vertical lines, creating a circular cylinder. The cross-sectional radius varies with $z$ in the cone because $z^2$ defines slanted boundaries, whereas in the cylinder, the complete absence of $z$ allows a fixed circle to extrude uniformly up and down.
